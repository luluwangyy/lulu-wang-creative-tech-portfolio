Choreographing Response

Investigating how bodily input and response timing shape participation in robotic environments.

Affiliation Barnard Movement Lab
Type Interactive Performance and Installation
Role Artist-Technologist
Duration October 2025–April 2026
Tools Python · Custom Robotics · ETC Eos · QLab · OSC · Raspberry Pi · ESP32

Research Question

How does the timing of a robotic environment’s response shape a participant’s understanding of their influence over it?

During my residency at the Barnard Movement Lab, I developed a series of connections between audience input, a custom robotic tentacle, and the lab’s theatrical lighting system. These experiments made it possible to explore interaction at two scales: the movement of an individual mechanical body and changes in the surrounding room.

The configurations differ in how an action produces a consequence. A clap can trigger movement immediately, or contribute to a sequence in which lighting changes precede robotic activation.

Immediate and Accumulated Responses

In the first configuration, Python-based sound detection identifies a clap and triggers the tentacle’s crawling movement. This establishes a direct relationship between an acoustic event and a visible mechanical response.

A clap triggers the robotic tentacle’s crawling movement, without lighting.

I then connected the system to the ETC Eos lighting console. Each clap changes the light color. On the thirteenth clap, the light turns red, its zoom reaches 100%, and the robotic tentacle begins to move.

Each clap changes the light color. The thirteenth clap turns the light red, brings its zoom to 100%, and activates the robotic tentacle.

Configuration Input and response Research focus
Immediate activation A detected clap triggers robotic movement. How people recognize a connection between their action and the robot.
Accumulated activation Claps change the lighting; the thirteenth initiates robotic movement. How people interpret an unfolding response and discover the conditions for activation.

The second configuration makes timing part of the encounter. Lighting registers successive actions while the robot remains still until the threshold is reached. This provides a setting for investigating anticipation, discoverability, and how people revise their actions as they encounter a system’s behavior.

For the development of the robotic mechanism, see The Roro.

Participation Across the Room

Clapping allows input without a handheld controller. It also introduces ambiguity in a shared space: several people may contribute to the same sequence, and the system responds to detected events without identifying who produced them.

This raises questions about collective participation. Can someone tell whether their clap caused a change? How might a group coordinate once it discovers the activation pattern? Does the delayed movement encourage continued experimentation or make the connection harder to understand?

Extension Through Touch

I also connected the robotic mechanism to Tactone, my ceramic musical interface. A designated sequence of notes triggers movement, extending the investigation from acoustic input to a physically arranged touch sequence.

Here, participants choose the order and timing of contact, while the resulting robotic movement remains choreographed. This creates another way to examine how people understand their role in initiating a performance.

A designated sequence played on the ceramic instrument triggers movement in the robotic mechanism.

Current Outcome and Further Inquiry

The project establishes working configurations connecting bodily input, robotic actuation, and theatrical lighting. Further investigation could examine how participants discover the mappings, attribute changes to their actions, and coordinate with others.

The central inquiry is how a responsive environment makes participation understandable: what people can influence, how that influence becomes visible, and how the encounter changes as they learn.

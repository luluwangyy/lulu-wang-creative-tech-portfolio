Tether

A remotely actuated robotic wearable investigating how a mechanical embrace mediates care, discomfort, and bodily agency across distance.

Type Wearable, Robotics, Interactive Prototype
Role Creative Technologist & Interaction Designer
Duration 2025
Tools Raspberry Pi · Microcontroller · Servo Motors · Wood Fabrication · Silkscreen Printing · Custom Software

How might a wearable robot translate a loved one’s remote gesture into a felt sense of presence?

This project grew from my experience living across continents, separated from my family by geography and time zones. The neck pillow I carried on repeated journeys of more than twenty hours became a starting point: an intimate object that supports the body while accompanying the experience of distance.

Louise Bourgeois’s Maman influenced the wearable’s form. I was drawn to how its spider-like body suggests both maternal protection and unease. Its enveloping structure informed Tether’s articulated wooden limbs. Worn around the neck, they bring this tension close to the body: an enclosing movement might resemble an embrace, while also making the wearer aware that someone else has initiated the contact.

I developed an eight-degree-of-freedom mechanism using servo motors, handcrafted wooden components, fabric, and custom software. Its limbs contract and extend around the neck. I explored two ways for my mother to activate it:

Interaction Robotic response Question it opens
She presses “dance”/ “hug” on a remote interface. The wearable performs a choreographed movement sequence. What does she intend to communicate by choosing when to send a movement?
The system detects her smile. The wearable performs a tickling movement. How does linking an expression to touch affect the experience of giving and receiving it?

The two interactions give her different relationships to the movement. Pressing “dance” is an explicit command. A smile may be spontaneous, or she may choose to smile to activate the wearable. The smile-to-tickle mapping introduces the possibility of teasing and play, but a smile does not necessarily express an intention to touch. That uncertainty became an important question for the project.

In both cases, I designed the choreography. My mother’s input determines when it begins, while the mechanism determines how that input reaches my body. The movement alone cannot tell me why she sent it. I am interested in how its meaning might develop through our conversations and repeated exchanges: whether a particular movement becomes a greeting, a joke, or a familiar way of asking for attention.

The neck-worn form also raises questions about bodily agency. Contact can be affectionate and still arrive at an unwanted moment. How should the wearer accept, interrupt, or decline a remotely initiated movement? How might the sender know whether it was welcome? These questions place the experience of receiving touch alongside the ability to send it.

Tether establishes a working prototype for exploring these exchanges. It connects remote button input and smile detection to two forms of robotic movement; whether those movements communicate care or a sense of presence remains to be investigated. The next stage is to develop the interaction vocabulary with my mother—comparing her intentions with my bodily experience, then revising the triggers, rhythms, and movements together.

Dimensions: 23.6 × 5.9 × 4.7 inches when extended; dimensions vary during contraction.
